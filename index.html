Invoice audit lab: can you spot what the checks catch?

In accounts payable, three things are worth checking every cycle: duplicate invoices, invoices paid late, and amounts that sit just under an approval limit. Below is a made-up ledger of 47 invoices with problems hidden in it.

Takes about 2 minutes. Short on time? Skip to the retail profitability study, or read my résumé.

Synthetic data. I generated this ledger for the demo. The vendors are fictional, and no employer or real vendor data is used.

    What each check does

    • Exact duplicate: same vendor, same invoice number, same amount.
    • Possible duplicate: same vendor and amount, different invoice number, invoice dates close together. A resubmitted invoice often gets a new number.
    • Just under limit: an amount below the approval limit but within a few percent of it.
    • Paid late: paid after the due date, plus any grace period you allow. Late payments can mean penalties.

    Scope and limits

    • The ledger is synthetic and the rules are simple. A real ledger also needs vendor master data, purchase order matching and payment records.
    • A flag is a lead for a person to check, not proof of an error.
    • The duplicate checks compare only vendor, invoice number, amount and date.
    what I learned

    What the lab taught me

    • Two kinds of duplicate need two kinds of check: an exact match is easy, but a repeat with a changed invoice number only shows up when you compare vendor, amount and date.
    • Thresholds change the answer. Widening the matching window from 7 to 5 days drops the flagged invoices from 12 to 10, so the setting is a judgment call to document, not a detail.
    • A flag is a lead for a person to look at, not proof of an error.

    Findings

    Observation

    With no discount, Furniture had a margin of 24.19%. Every discount level from 30% upward in the table lost money overall.

    Why it matters

    Higher discounts go together with lower profit. That's an association, not proof the discounts caused it. Product mix, cost and order size could explain some of it.

    What I'd do

    Look at how Furniture discounts get approved where margins went negative, and weigh the margin against the extra sales.

    Observation

    Tables was the only reviewed subcategory with a loss: 64,083.39 on sales of 757,034.

    Why it matters

    A loss can hide inside a healthy category total. These numbers show where to look, not why.

    What I'd do

    Break Tables down by product, discount, region, customer segment and time period. Check costs, product mix and shipping.

    Observation

    Furniture sold more than Office Supplies but earned less profit. Technology led on sales, profit and margin.

    Why it matters

    Sales alone don't tell you how a category is doing.

    What I'd do

    Track margin next to sales. Before changing pricing or discount policy, compare similar products and check how many orders sit behind each level.

    My first move

    Review every Furniture order discounted 30% or more. Every level from there lost money in this data, so that's where I'd start looking for the cause.

    Scope and limits

    • Exploratory analysis. The link between discounts and profit is an association.
    • I used only the fields in the dataset. It covers 2011 to 2014, so it may not reflect today or other businesses.
    • The dashboard covers category-level sales, profit and margin. Trends over time, regions and the cause of the Tables loss need more analysis.
    what I learned

    What this case taught me

    • Averages hide the story. Furniture's 6.98% overall margin hides discount levels that lose money, which only shows when you break it down.
    • A pattern is not a cause. Higher discounts go with lower margins here, but product mix and order size could explain part of it.
    • Re-checking my own numbers found a one-cent rounding difference, which is exactly the kind of thing worth knowing before someone else finds it.
